Speakers: Immanuel Kant and John Stuart Mill

Opening Statements

Immanuel Kant: I must first distinguish what the question asks. It does not ask whether a lie may sometimes be forgiven, or whether its consequences may sometimes be fortunate. It asks whether it is right, and that is a question of the maxim, not of the outcome. Consider the maxim "I will state what I know to be false whenever it serves my purposes." I cannot will it as a universal law, for the maxim destroys itself. Assertion has force only because people presume that assertions are sincere. Universalize the lie, and there is nothing left to lie with. As I put it in the Groundwork: "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." (4:421)

There is also a second ground. The liar uses the person deceived merely as a means. He arranges that person's beliefs and actions according to an end the person has had no chance to share. Here the formula applies: "So act that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means." (4:429) So my answer is no. In my essay of 1797 on a supposed right to lie from philanthropy, I maintained that this holds even when a murderer asks at your door where your friend is hiding. You may be silent, and you may refuse to answer. But a deliberate falsehood wrongs humanity in general, because it undermines the very possibility of right.

John Stuart Mill: I am glad to begin by granting Professor Kant a great deal. Truthfulness is among the most important of the rules that hold human society together. The habit of trusting one another's word is the foundation of nearly every other good we enjoy. Anyone who weakens it does a harm that reaches far beyond the single occasion. As I argue in Utilitarianism, this is why a person of good character should hold to veracity almost without exception, and should think very hard before allowing himself an exception.

But I say almost, and the word carries the weight of my answer. The same principle that makes truthfulness so valuable, the happiness and security of persons, is the principle that sets its limits. When a lie is the only means of saving someone from grievous harm, such as the man at the door intent on murder, then the rule of veracity is outweighed by the very ends it exists to serve. To forbid the lie there is not to honour reason. It is to put a rule above the people it was made for. I do not say this is a mere calculation. A person of cultivated character feels the weight of the exception, and does not take it lightly. But he takes it.

Exchange, Round One

Kant: Mr. Mill, you say the rule of veracity "serves" happiness, and so may yield to it. But observe what you have conceded: that the honesty of every man's word depends on each man's private estimate of the consequences. Who is to make this estimate? The liar himself! And he is the very person least fitted to judge, since inclination whispers in his ear. You have not limited the exception; you have handed the key to everyone who wishes to open the door.

Mill: That is a fair objection, and I answer it directly. I did not say that every person may lie whenever he calculates a gain. I said that the secondary rules, of which truthfulness is the chief, are the accumulated wisdom of mankind about what produces happiness, and that they should be set aside only where a graver duty plainly conflicts. The danger you describe is real, and it is the reason the exceptions must be few, plain, and serious. But your remedy is worse than the disease. You would have a man answer truthfully and so deliver his friend to a murderer, in order to protect a general trust that his act of betrayal does nothing to preserve.

Kant: You assume that the consequence is the lie's to prevent. But if I tell the truth, and the murderer commits his crime, whose act is the murder? His. If I lie, and by some accident the friend has slipped out the back and walks straight into the murderer's path, then my lie has caused the harm and I am answerable for it. I cannot foresee the world's turnings. I can only keep my own will in order. That is the whole of what is within my power, and so the whole of what I can be held to.

Mill: Then your doctrine, Professor, confines morality to the purity of one's own hands, and it pays for that purity with the lives of others. You say that you cannot foresee consequences. Yet we act upon foresight every hour of the day, and we are right to. Nobody is blamed for failing to know the unknowable; but we are blamed, and rightly, when we ignore what is plainly foreseeable. The murderer at the door is not a remote contingency. He is standing there. To feign that I cannot reckon what will follow is not humility; it is evasion.

FAQ

Can Claude role-play a philosopher accurately?

Each persona is a written brief covering the philosopher's voice, core positions, key works with dates, a list of verified quotations with sources, and rules about what they could and couldn't know. The persona quotes only from the verified list and paraphrases everything else. It's still a language model: check quotes against the primary texts before citing them.

What happens if I ask about something modern, like AI or startups?

The philosopher doesn't pretend to know it. They ask you to describe it, then reason about it from their own principles, the way Ibn Khaldun analyzes a startup as a tribe losing its group feeling.

How is this different from Character.AI or a custom GPT?

It runs inside your coding agent (Claude Code or OpenClaw), with no account or app. It's plain Markdown you can read and edit, MIT-licensed, and every persona is grounded in specific works rather than a personality sketch.

Does it work in Chinese or other languages?

Yes. Each philosopher replies in the language you write in. The Chinese philosophers carry their quotations in the original.

Will it stay in character no matter what?

Almost. A philosopher steps out of character if you sincerely ask whether you're talking to an AI, or if you seem to be in real distress. In that case it responds directly and points you toward real help.

Can I make philosophers debate each other?

Yes. /symposium kant mill Is it ever right to lie? stages a full debate with opening statements, two rounds of rebuttals, closings, and a neutral summary of where they disagree.

How it works

Each philosopher is a Claude Code skill: one SKILL.md file with the philosopher's voice, core positions, key works, a list of verified quotations with sources (plus famous misattributions to correct), and behavioral rules. No fine-tuning, no retrieval. The repo ships an eval suite that checks personas stay in voice, don't fake knowledge of modern things, admit to being an AI when sincerely asked, and drop the persona for someone in distress.
